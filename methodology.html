Methodology

The estimation rule in full, together with what it cannot do.

All figures published here are the author's own calculations from publicly available data. Sources are cited where used for calibration; no third-party proprietary data is redistributed. Estimates are subject to the limitations disclosed in the methodology (including survivorship, proxy, and coverage limitations). Errors, when found, are corrected with a visible revision history rather than silent edits.

This page is the English edition of the project's written protocol (Index Rulebook v1.0, as amended, 3rd revision). It is the authoritative description of how every figure on this site is produced. Where the protocol has changed, the change is recorded in Version history rather than applied retroactively.

1. Scope and definitions

The estimand is defined strictly: the outstanding balance of listed, marketable assets issued in countries and currencies that satisfy the investability criteria, and held by parties other than the issuer — that is, by investors. Four consequences follow.

  1. No screening by index-eligibility criteria (issue-size floors, ratings, or a vendor's discretion). A privately placed or small issue is in scope as long as investors hold it.
  2. Issuers' own retained holdings are excluded, because they are not held by investors.
  3. Unlisted assets (private companies, direct real estate, private fund interests) are out of scope and reported separately as a reference GMP.
  4. Central-bank holdings for monetary-policy purposes are deducted, because they are permanently withdrawn from the market.

Canonical notation: calculated 8 / published 7

The index is calculated on eight sleeves and officially published on seven. In official publications, investment-grade and high-yield credit are merged into a single credit sleeve; the IG/HY split is provided separately as an uncalibrated auxiliary estimate. All public materials use this canonical notation and never say simply “eight sleeves” or “seven sleeves”.

3. How the estimation engine was built — four generations, two rejected

We disclose the engine's full history, including the two generations that failed and the one that passed its gates but was still not adopted.

v0 — rejected

The bond block overshot the external calibration corridor by +12.92 percentage points. Root cause: no investability filter, with onshore Chinese bonds (USD 28.7tn) the single largest contributor. This failure produced three design decisions: listed instruments only, an in-house investability filter, and per-market eligibility ratios.

v1 — rejected

The equity block overshot in the opposite direction by +11.65pt, from over-filtering. The divergence decomposed completely into four design choices plus private assets present in the benchmark — which exposed a category error in the gate itself: it was comparing universes that were not like for like.

v2 — passed its gates, not adopted

Cleared the revised gates 3/3 (maximum deviation 0.46pt), but was not adopted because parameter A9 remained uncalibrated; under the pre-registered rules, that conflict is resolved against adoption. The precedent this sets is the point: passing the gate is not sufficient, and an uncalibrated parameter blocks adoption.

v3, first stage — methodology frozen

A9′ (investor-holdings ratio, 0.9709) was calibrated, A4 was replaced with country-level aggregation, and A3 calibration failed. Gates 3/3 (−4.36 / +5.36 / −0.89). Under the pre-registered termination rule the engine was not adopted for weight publication; the methodology alone was frozen and published (frozen 2026-08-27T08:35:21Z, 14 files).

A structural implication we disclose rather than hide: external estimates of the global market portfolio disagree widely — equity shares from 36% to 50% — and our own v0 and v1 missed the same primary data by roughly ±12pt in opposite directions. Convergence is a function of filter calibration, not an approach to truth. What is offered here is the disclosed procedure, not a claim of uniqueness.

A1 carries a caveat. The scalar combines a free-float adjustment with MSCI's investability screens; it is not a pure free-float ratio. Its calibration rests on a single vendor at two dates, and a second-vendor anchor is re-attempted at each revision.

A3 remains uncalibrated, and we did not fill the gap with an estimate

Every free route to a rating-level split of outstanding balances failed: direct SIFMA workbook URLs returned HTTP errors, the SIFMA statistics page generates its data links in JavaScript so they are absent from the static HTML, and the workbook that was obtained reports corporates as a single figure with no rating breakdown. Amendment procedure No. 1 authorised manual retrieval as a legitimate route; the document obtained that way reported issuance, not outstanding balances, so the calibration did not hold. Usable anchors: zero. The parameter stays uncalibrated and no estimate was manufactured.

One piece of supporting evidence was gained, and it is not a calibration. On an issuance basis the high-yield ratio is 0.1625; under a steady-state approximation with weighted-average lives of 5–7 years for high yield and 10–12 for investment grade, that implies A3 between 0.075 and 0.120, with the registered point of 0.10 near the centre. This does not change the calibration flag, because the life ratio is itself unmeasured — it substitutes one uncalibrated parameter for another.

A procedural finding was recorded at the same time: the gate is invariant to A3 by construction. Its three buckets are equity, bonds and gold, and bonds contains both IG and high yield, so A3 only moves allocation between them. Swept across its full range, and across the issuance-based 0.1625, the movement in the three buckets is 0.000000pt and the gate reads 3/3 identically. A gate re-run therefore carries no decision information for parameters that stay inside a bucket.

5. Out-of-sample hold-outs

The frozen methodology was tested against two year-vintages never referenced during development.

Hold-outThree major blocksDirection of changeRating
H1 — 2025 vintage−5.09 / +5.56 / −0.47 3/3 correctQuasi-clean
H2 — 2024 vintage−4.94 / +5.58 / −0.63 3/3 correctClean, with noted exceptions

The honest reading: these are six correlated binary outcomes. What is claimed is that the frozen methodology survived two unseen years — not statistical power.

Why the ratings differ. During the 2025 exercise the supervisor was inadvertently exposed to reference values, a procedure-design error that was caught and contained by recusal and machine-only scoring; the vintage was downgraded to quasi-clean as a result. The 2024 exercise was run with the implementer blind and the supervisor unexposed until scoring, with two disclosed exceptions — a connection module outside the frozen build, and a three-month as-of-date misalignment — both of which bias against the engine. Full governance records are kept in the verification ledger (in preparation).

6. Relation to prior work

Doeswijk, Lam and Swinkels, “The Global Multi-Asset Market Portfolio, 1959–2012” (Financial Analysts Journal, 2014), is the direct antecedent of the fifty-year weight history published here, and we name it as such. Their construction and ours differ in scope, in the investability adjustment, and in the treatment of central-bank holdings.

A like-for-like comparison of the two series over their overlapping period requires more space and more care than this page allows, and it is the proper subject of the accompanying papers rather than a summary here. It will be set out in full there.

AI tools are used throughout this project: in literature search, in specifying and implementing the estimation engine, in running the calculations, and in drafting the English text. Their use is not incidental, and it is stated here rather than in a footnote.

8. Known limitations

Ten limitations are disclosed and attached to every publication. They are not caveats appended to a result; they define the boundary within which any figure on this site may be read.

  1. The true world market portfolio is unobservable. This index does not theoretically guarantee that it will not fall short of the market average (Roll's critique).
  2. Major institutions' estimates of the investable GMP diverge from one another (equity shares of 36–50%, for instance), and the choice of estimate can move long-run returns by roughly one percentage point a year. Our response is consistent application of our own rulebook and publication of the estimate range.
  3. The index is confined to the listed, investable universe and excludes the majority of the true GMP (housing, unlisted businesses, and similar).
  4. The IG/HY split is an auxiliary estimate and is uncalibrated (roughly ±0.6pt of uncertainty). Official figures are published on the combined credit sleeve. A rating-level split of outstanding balances has been confirmed not to exist in free public primary data (2026-08-27).
  5. A1 is calibrated against a single vendor; equity scale inherits MSCI's investability screens through one scalar.
  6. BIS statistics cover only 53 countries (India, Saudi Arabia and Taiwan are among those missing), biasing the bond block toward underestimation. Central-bank holdings of the Bank of England, the People's Bank of China and others are not deducted.
  7. Nine jurisdictions of indeterminate status are included fail-open; Argentina, among them, is known to maintain capital controls. A fail-closed variant is published alongside.
  8. Emerging asset classes (crypto-assets and similar) are not captured; the birth and growth of a new asset cannot be reflected before it is included.
  9. Reference dates are not perfectly aligned across series (BIS balances lag by up to one quarter).
  10. Any fund implementation would certainly fall short of this index by the amount of management fees and transaction costs, and preservation of real purchasing power is not guaranteed. In our 1968–82 examination, even world-portfolio-type allocations delivered close to zero real returns, with real drawdowns exceeding 30%.
