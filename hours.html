Measuring Assets in Hours of the World’s Work

Summary

Every asset price is a ratio: something over a unit. The unit is almost always a currency, and every currency loses value against the things people ultimately want. This note proposes and tests a different unit — nominal GDP per hour worked, aggregated across countries at GDP weights, referred to here as the world hour — and re-measures a half-century of asset prices in it.

Four results follow.

  1. The world hour behaves like the other non-gold units and differs from them in what it leaves out. Against a GDP-weighted currency basket, consumer prices, manufacturing wages and broad money per person, its volatility in USD terms sits in the same band (3.4–4.5% a year) and is not notably low within it; gold is four times noisier than any of them. Its drift (5.7% a year over 1968–2026) lies between consumer prices and money. It is not free of prices, exchange rates or politics. What it is free of is two things the other units carry — the distribution of income between labour and capital, and asset-market expectations — and that, not a variance statistic, is the case for it.
  2. Dividing by GDP and dividing by GDP per hour are different questions. Total hours worked grew about 0.7% a year across the panel since 2014 and about 0.95% a year over 1950–2023. An asset divided by GDP asks “what share of production”; divided by GDP per hour it asks “how many hours of work”. The gap compounds.
  3. In world hours, the post-2014 rally is smaller than it looks in dollars. From 2014-Q1 to 2026-Q2 the S&P 500 with dividends reinvested rose 5.0× in dollars and 3.5× in world hours; gold rose 2.5× in world hours. Between a fifth (in log terms) and two-fifths (in arithmetic terms) of the dollar gain is the rise in what an hour of the world’s work is worth. Over 2000–2025 the S&P 500 in world hours is about 2.7× its starting level, against 7.1× in dollars.
  4. The global market portfolio held since 2020 is roughly flat in world hours. Measured from 2019-Q4, a rule-based GMP index reached 107 in world-hour terms by 2026-Q2 after a trough of 83 in 2022-Q3. Over the long run both world equities and the GMP have beaten the world hour: in every rolling 20-year window since the 1970s the excess was positive, with a median of 4.3% a year for equities and 3.0% for the GMP.

The unit does not replace the dollar. It answers a question the dollar cannot: whether wealth has grown relative to the work that produces everything wealth can buy.

1. Why a unit of account is a design choice

The first note in this series (Nakanishi 2026a) built an index of the investable global market portfolio and reported all results in US dollars, stating that no claims were made for other numeraires. That reservation was deliberate. A fifty-year return series in dollars mixes three things: the change in the asset, the change in the dollar against other currencies, and the change in what a dollar buys. Deflating by consumer prices removes some of the third but introduces a basket whose composition, quality adjustments and hedonics are themselves contested, and which by construction ignores productivity: a unit that holds a basket of goods fixed will always show real assets “outrunning” it as production per person grows.

Ricardo’s (1823) last, unfinished essay concluded that no invariable measure of value exists, and that any unit must be chosen for the question at hand. The question this note asks is the one a long-horizon saver asks: has my claim on the world’s output grown or shrunk relative to the work that produces it? The natural unit for that question is an hour of work, valued at what the world actually pays for it in aggregate — not the wage, which depends on the labour share, but output per hour.

Keynes (1936, ch. 4) took the wage unit as his measure precisely because it was the most stable available; Tupy and Pooley (2018–2022) use nominal GDP per hour worked as a proxy for global time income in their “time-price” measure of abundance; Officer and Williamson’s MeasuringWorth project reports historical values in several units side by side and refuses to select one. This note sits in that line. What it adds is a reproducible, GDP-weighted, chained world series at quarterly frequency, a comparison of its statistical properties with five alternatives, and its application to asset prices — including the GMP built in the first note.

2. Definition

For each country c with a local-currency index I and an exchange rate FX (US dollars per unit of local currency), the chained USD-denominated world unit P is

Δlog P_t = Σ_c s_{c,t} · [ Δlog I_{c,t} + Δlog FX_{c,t} ] / Σ_c s_{c,t}

where s_{c,t} is the country’s share of the prior year’s nominal GDP in US dollars (World Bank), re-normalised each period over the countries with data in that period.

The candidate units, all built with the same weights and the same chain, differ only in I:

Unit Country index I
Currency basket 1 (exchange rate only)
Consumer prices CPI
Wages Manufacturing hourly earnings
Money per person Broad money ÷ population
World hour Nominal GDP ÷ total hours worked
Gold USD price (no country dimension)

For comparison, a composite unit is also defined as a geometric mean, log U = Σ w_i log P_i, in two equal-weight presets: one using wages (equal5), one using the world hour (equal5t). An asset’s value in world hours is its USD price divided by the world-hour index. Where a proper name is needed, the world-hour series is referred to as the CC-WH (Capital Census World Hour) index; “world hour” in the text is the generic term. Where the note reports a value in “any unit”, the minimum and maximum across the six units are shown as a disagreement band.

Two design decisions deserve a sentence each. The weights are nominal GDP at market exchange rates, not purchasing-power parity, because the object being measured is what the world pays for an hour at the prices at which assets trade; Section 8 reports the PPP-weighted alternative. And the series is a chain index: it measures the change in the value of an hour holding the country mix fixed period to period, so the shift of hours from higher- to lower-output countries appears in the level (Section 4.3) but not in the chained change.

The panel is 44 countries and territories. The monthly series uses the 43 with World Bank annual GDP; the quarterly series and the levels in Section 4.3 use the 38 with both OECD quarterly GDP and an hours source (Hong Kong, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand have hours but no quarterly GDP). Handling rules, stated so they can be disputed: BIS exchange-rate series for euro-area members are already expressed in euro before 1999, so mark-to-euro chaining is needed only for the euro-area monetary aggregate; series without an official seasonal adjustment are adjusted by ratio to a centred moving average; China and Saudi Arabia lack hours-per-worker data, so 2,100 hours per worker a year are assumed for the level calculation (this does not enter the chained index); months after the last annual observation are extrapolated at the trailing one-year growth rate and flagged provisional; the latest quarter is dropped if the reporting countries cover less than half of panel GDP, and reported as provisional when they cover less than all of it (currently about 80%).

4. Properties of the unit

4.1 Drift and volatility

Table 1 reports, for each unit in USD, the annualised drift and the annualised standard deviation of log changes — monthly changes × √12 for 1968–2026, quarterly changes × 2 for 2014–2026. The two columns are not the same object for the world hour: the long column is the annually interpolated monthly series (which mechanically smooths it, and which is provisional after 2025-06), the short column the quarterly series observed from OECD data.

For the US alone, where no exchange-rate component enters, the same figures are CPI 3.9/1.1, wages 4.1/1.0, money per person 5.6/1.7, gold 8.0/16.1 and the equal-weight composite 4.3/3.3.

Two readings. First, the five non-gold units have almost the same volatility (3.4–4.5%), most of it the exchange-rate component they share; the world hour is not distinguished within that band — the currency basket is marginally lower over 1968–2026, and over 2014–2026 the world hour is the highest of the five. Its long-sample figure is also flattered by interpolation. No claim of superior stability is made on this evidence. Second, over 1968–2026 the drifts order the units as national accounting would predict: cash below prices, prices below wages, wages below output per hour (the labour share fell), output per hour below money (velocity fell), and gold above everything with four times the noise.

The case for the world hour is therefore structural, not statistical, and is worth stating explicitly. Nominal GDP per hour contains prices, exchange rates and hours — it is not a “real” measure. What it does not contain is the split of output between wages and profits, and it does not contain the market’s expectation of the future. Those two omissions are exactly what make wages, money and gold move more than it does.

4.2 GDP and GDP per hour are not the same denominator

It is tempting to treat an asset-to-GDP ratio as the same thing as an asset in hours. It is not. The difference is total hours worked, which grow with population and participation and shrink with recessions and shorter working weeks.

Table 2. World panel, chained, 2014-Q1 = 100 (author’s calculations)

Nominal GDP GDP per hour Total hours
2020-Q2 98 104 94
2026-Q1 153 141 109
Annualised 3.6% 2.9% 0.7%

In 2020-Q2 GDP fell and GDP per hour rose, because hours fell faster than output. Over the 22-country long sample (1950–2023) hours grew 0.95% a year; over 2014–2025 the annual rate was +1.0% in the United States, 0.0 in Japan, +0.2 in Germany, +0.9 in the UK, −0.1 in Korea, −0.3 in China, +2.6 in India, +0.6 in Brazil, +1.5 in Mexico and +1.8 in Indonesia. A ratio to GDP therefore understates how many hours an asset commands in countries where hours are growing, and the gap widens as the emerging-market weight rises.

4.3 The level: what is an hour of the world’s work worth?

The chained index has no level; the level comes from the panel ratio, nominal GDP in USD divided by total hours. It was $15.3 in 2014-Q1 and $20.7 in 2026-Q2. Country levels in 2026-Q1: United States 106, Germany 88, Japan 40, Korea 39, Saudi Arabia 37, Mexico 16, China 14, Brazil 13, Indonesia 5, India 2 (USD per hour). The level includes the shift in composition toward lower-output hours; the chained change does not. Both are reported because they answer different questions.

4.4 Output per hour versus the wage

In the United States, GDP per hour has outrun manufacturing hourly earnings by roughly 20% since 2000 — the labour-share decline in one number. The world gap is larger, but it mixes two things: a distributional shift, and a composition effect, because the wage panel is OECD-only and excludes China and India while the GDP-per-hour panel includes them. The two should not be read as the same phenomenon.

5.3 Rolling excess of world equities and the GMP over the world hour

World equities are the equity sleeve of the frozen backtest engine behind the first note (USD, dividends reinvested, February 1972 to June 2025); the GMP is that note’s rulebook-compliant index (January 1976 to June 2025). Both are taken from the frozen engine’s output files, whose checksums are given in the Appendix. The world hour here is the monthly (annually interpolated) series. Table 5 reports the distribution of annualised excess returns over the world hour across all rolling windows.

Worst 10th pct Median 90th pct Share of windows negative

The windows overlap month by month — 521 ten-year and 401 twenty-year windows for equities, 474 and 354 for the GMP, of which only about five and two respectively are independent — so “share of windows negative” is a historical frequency, not a probability. Over the whole sample world equities beat the world hour by 4.0% a year. The worst ten-year windows end in February 2009 and July 1982 — the two occasions on which falling equities coincided with a fast-rising world hour (inflation). The GMP’s floor is higher and its ceiling lower, as its bond and gold sleeves would predict.

A practical translation: an investor whose wealth has fallen 34% in world hours needs an excess return of 4.2% a year over ten years, 2.8% over fifteen or 2.1% over twenty to recover it. Historically, world equities delivered at least that in 61%, 70% and 91% of windows respectively. Recovery in hours is likely at twenty years and far from certain at ten.

In dollars the GMP looks like a 45% gain over six and a half years. In hours it is a 17% drawdown to 2022-Q3, still −12% at end-2022, then a recovery to +7% — six and a half years inside a band from −17% to +7%, ending close to flat. The reason is structural: a GDP-weighted world unit and a market-weighted world portfolio share most of their currency composition, so the exchange-rate noise that dominates Table 1 cancels.

5.5 What should be flat in world hours

A first-principles list, reported as hypotheses rather than findings:

  • A claim on nominal GDP itself is flat by construction. Equity is such a claim plus a risk premium and plus the labour-share effect, so it should — and, in Table 5, does — outrun the hour over long horizons.
  • Inflation-linked bonds lose the productivity term, roughly 2% a year.
  • Commodities’ time prices fall 3–4% a year over the long run (Tupy and Pooley); they cannot be flat in hours. Gold is the exception in the sample, and the 1968 start (the year the two-tier gold market began) flatters it.
  • Cash bears the full depreciation of its currency, between −2% and −8% a year across the panel.

6. Currencies in world hours

Every currency must lose value against the world hour, because the hour’s value grows with both prices and productivity. The US dollar fell from 100 to 71 between 2014-Q2 and 2026-Q2, −2.8% a year. Over the same period the GDP-weighted currency basket lost 2.2% a year against the dollar and 5.0% a year against the world hour; over the long sample since 1968 the basket’s loss against the dollar is 2.5% a year. US nominal GDP per hour has grown 4.2% a year since 2014; the world hour, in dollars, 2.9%. The difference is the dollar’s appreciation and the panel’s weight toward lower-growth currencies.

7. Where this sits in the literature

The author’s literature file organises about 90 items under nine headings; the ones that bear directly on the design are these. Keynes (1936) chose the wage unit for stability. Tupy and Pooley (2018–2022) defined the time price and proxied global time income with nominal GDP per hour. Officer and Williamson (MeasuringWorth) refused a single measure and report several. Hall (1982) proposed a target-tracking commodity basket as a unit. Long (1990) defined the numeraire portfolio — the portfolio in whose units every asset is a martingale — which is the closest finance-theoretic relative of what Section 5.4 finds empirically for the GMP. Ricardo (1823) supplies the epistemic caveat. Three units with substantial literatures were not implemented here and are the obvious next steps: a commodity basket, the market portfolio itself as numeraire, and energy.

Three conclusions.

The measurement choices are second-order. Replacing the four-tier hours construction with a single method for every country moves the end point by at most 1.4 points; the seasonal-adjustment method moves it by nothing (STL was used as the alternative; X-13 was not available in the build environment, and the STL result is close enough to make the test unnecessary). The PWT-interpolated variant has the same end point but is noisier quarter to quarter (correlation 0.86), which is what interpolating annual data should do. The independent annual build in world.py, from World Bank GDP and Penn World Table hours, lands within half a point of the quarterly series at 2025-Q2. The hours data are the weakest input, but the ways of handling them do not change the answer.

Weights are first-order but interpretable. PPP weights raise the end point by three points and the growth rate by a quarter of a percent a year, because they give more weight to countries whose output per hour is growing faster in dollars. That is the expected direction and size, and it does not change any ordering in Table 1. Equal weights are not a serious alternative — they give Indonesia the weight of the United States — and are shown only to bound the sensitivity.

Composition is what matters, and specifically China. Every variant that drops countries lowers the index: excluding China takes off eight points, restricting to advanced economies five, restricting to the 23 countries with official quarterly hours fourteen. These are not measurement error; they are different objects. A world hour built on OECD members only is an OECD hour, and it grows 1.7% a year rather than 2.9%. The “officially adjusted series only” row belongs to the same family: the two countries it drops are China and Saudi Arabia (their nominal GDP is published unadjusted), while Canada, Chile, Mexico and New Zealand stay in with a different hours source — so its −7.9% is, in effect, the China exclusion again. China matters because its output per hour in dollars grew faster than the panel average. Note the direction of the remaining bias: China’s hours per worker are held at a fixed 2,100 a year for lack of data, and they have in fact been falling, so the baseline most likely understates China’s growth in output per hour. Including China more accurately would push the world hour up, not down; the baseline is on the conservative side.

For Sections 5 and 6 the reading is this: the end-of-sample level of the unit moves by about ±3% under the weighting choice and about ±8% under the treatment of China. Neither changes the order of magnitude of any result — the S&P 500 at 3.5× and gold at 2.5× in world hours, the GMP roughly flat since 2020, every currency depreciating — and the direction of every result holds under all ten variants.

9. Limitations

Stated in the order of their likely importance.

The unit is nominal and includes exchange rates. The world hour is not a “real” quantity. It is a statement of what the world pays, in dollars, for an hour of work at the prices at which assets trade. A reader who wants a productivity measure should use hours at constant prices, which is a different object.

Hours data are the weakest link. Quarterly total hours are observed for 23 countries; the rest are constructed from employment and annual hours, or interpolated from annual Penn World Table data. China’s hours per worker are held at a fixed value throughout, which Section 8 argues biases the unit downward; an ILO-based estimate is the obvious replacement. The seasonal adjustment used for non-adjusted series is crude. Section 8 shows that these choices move the end point by less than two points; the panel composition moves it by up to fourteen.

The latest quarters are provisional. The most recent quarter is computed on the countries that have reported (about 80% of panel GDP) and is revised as the rest arrive. Months after the last annual observation are extrapolated. The note reports the provisional values because withholding them would hide the reason the series changes.

Vintages were not tested. All series are the current published vintage; a rebuild on data as first published, to measure how much the latest quarters are revised, has not been done.

One history. The distributions in Table 5 are drawn from a single realisation, 1972–2025, whose long-window results depend heavily on the 1980–2000 equity expansion. They describe what happened, not what the distribution of outcomes is.

The wage comparison mixes composition with distribution. Section 4.4 cannot separate the labour-share decline from the fact that the wage panel excludes the two largest emerging economies.

Weights are one convention. Market-rate GDP weights were chosen for the reason given in Section 2; PPP weights are an equally defensible convention and give a series about three points higher at the end of the sample (Section 8).

No claim of usefulness as a target. The world hour is proposed as a measuring unit, not as a policy target, an indexation base or an investment benchmark. Nothing here suggests holding any asset because of its behaviour in world hours.
